## A. Marcus Aurelius / Daily Stoic Reflection

> *"When you wake up in the morning, tell yourself: the people I deal with today will be meddling, ungrateful, arrogant, dishonest, jealous and surly. They are this way because they cannot tell good from evil. But I have seen the beauty of good, and the ugliness of evil, and have recognized that the wrongdoer has a nature related to my own."*
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations*, Book II

**What he meant:** Marcus wrote this not as a cynical complaint but as a morning inoculation — a way to prepare himself emotionally so that other people's behaviour wouldn't throw him off course. He was Emperor of Rome, dealing with senators, soldiers, and courtiers daily, and he knew that expecting perfection from others was a recipe for constant frustration.

**Practical application today:** Before you check your phone or walk into any interaction today, pause and accept that someone will likely disappoint or irritate you. That's not pessimism — it's preparation. When it happens, you can meet it with patience instead of surprise.

**Practice for today:** When someone frustrates you today, silently say: *"They are doing the best they can with what they know."* Then choose your response from that place.

## B. AA Daily Reflection — June 6

**Title: ALL WE DO IS TRY**

> *"Can He now take them all — every one?"*
> — Alcoholics Anonymous, p. 76

**Message:** Step Six doesn't demand perfection — it asks for willingness. Not all character defects disappear at once, and some may linger for a lifetime. What the program asks is that we name our defects honestly, claim them as our own rather than blaming circumstances, and be willing to let go of the ones we can — just for today. As we grow in recovery, many of our old patterns become more uncomfortable to us, which itself becomes the invitation to revisit this step. Progress, not perfection.

## C. Bible Verse of the Day

**1 Corinthians 10:24 (NIV)**

> *"No one should seek their own good, but the good of others."*

**Context:** Paul wrote this letter to the church at Corinth around 55 AD, addressing a divided and self-focused congregation. The surrounding passage deals with whether Christians should eat food that had been offered to idols — a major social and spiritual question of the day. Paul's answer cuts to a deeper principle: the question isn't just "is this allowed for me?" but "does this help my neighbour?" This verse became a cornerstone of early Christian ethics — the idea that freedom in Christ is meant to be exercised *for* others, not merely *for ourselves*.

## D. Merriam-Webster Word of the Day

**valedictory** *(adjective)* \val-uh-DIK-tuh-ree\

**Definition:** Expressing or containing a farewell; of or relating to a valediction.

**Example sentence:** *At the close of her final board meeting, she offered a valedictory address that moved even her sharpest critics.*

**Etymology:** From New Latin *valedictōrius*, rooted in the Latin *vale dīcere* — literally "to say farewell" (*vale* = be well, *dīcere* = to say). The word entered English in the mid-1600s, first appearing in religious contexts ("valedictory sermon") and later became attached to academic traditions. By the time Noah Webster compiled his 1828 dictionary, the *valedictory address* was already an established graduation institution in American colleges. The student who delivers it — the *valedictorian* — literally "says farewell" on behalf of the class.

## E. This Day in History — June 6

**1944 — D-Day, Operation Overlord**
Nearly 160,000 Allied troops crossed the English Channel in the largest seaborne invasion in history, landing on five beaches in Normandy, France. The operation involved 5,000 ships and 13,000 aircraft. It was the turning point that began the liberation of Western Europe from Nazi Germany — and it was planned under conditions of extraordinary secrecy, with an elaborate disinformation campaign (Operation Bodyguard) convincing Hitler the real invasion would come at Calais.

**1844 — The YMCA is Founded**
George Williams founded the Young Men's Christian Association in London, originally as a refuge for young men moving to cities during the Industrial Revolution — a place for Bible study and prayer away from the dangers of street life. It has since grown into one of the largest nonprofit organizations in the world, serving over 64 million people annually.

**1523 — Sweden Gets Its First Modern King**
Gustav Vasa was elected King of Sweden, ending Danish rule and establishing the independent Swedish state. June 6 is still celebrated as Sweden's National Day. Vasa also broke with the Catholic Church and established Lutheranism as the state religion — reshaping Scandinavian culture for centuries.

**1889 — The Great Seattle Fire**
A cabinet-maker's glue pot tipped over and within hours had destroyed 25 city blocks in downtown Seattle. Rather than being broken by the disaster, the city rebuilt on a grid raised several feet above the original street level — which is why Seattle has famous underground tunnels beneath its streets today.

**1966 — James Meredith Shot During March Against Fear**
Civil rights activist James Meredith, who had become the first Black student to attend the University of Mississippi in 1962, was shot just one day into his solo 220-mile march through Mississippi to encourage Black voter registration. His wounding galvanized the civil rights movement; other leaders including Martin Luther King Jr. and Stokely Carmichael took up the march in his place, drawing national attention.

**1985 — Remains of Josef Mengele Identified**
Skeletal remains exhumed from a grave in Brazil were confirmed through forensic analysis to be those of Josef Mengele, the Nazi SS officer known as the "Angel of Death" for his brutal experiments on prisoners at Auschwitz. He had evaded capture for 35 years after fleeing to South America.

## F. Britannica Fact

**The Eiffel Tower grows in summer.**

The Eiffel Tower, built in 1889 as a temporary structure for the Paris World's Fair, was originally despised by many Parisians who called it an "iron monstrosity." But here's what most people don't know: it is actually *taller* in summer than in winter. Due to thermal expansion, the iron structure grows by up to 15 centimetres (about 6 inches) on hot days as the metal expands in the heat. The tower was designed to be dismantled after 20 years — it survived only because it proved useful as a radio transmission tower. Today it is the most-visited paid monument in the world, with nearly 7 million visitors a year.
