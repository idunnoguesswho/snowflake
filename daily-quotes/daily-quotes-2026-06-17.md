## A. Marcus Aurelius / Daily Stoic Reflection

> *"You have power over your mind — not outside events. Realize this, and you will find strength."*
> — Marcus Aurelius, Meditations

**What he meant:** Marcus was constantly surrounded by chaos — wars, plagues, political intrigues, ungrateful people. His point was not that external things don't happen to us, but that we are not obligated to be *disturbed* by them. The Stoics drew a firm line between what is "up to us" (our judgments, reactions, intentions) and what is not (other people, outcomes, the weather of life).

**Practical application today:** When something frustrates you — a task that doesn't go as planned, a conversation that goes sideways — pause before reacting. Ask: *"Is this within my control?"* If not, release the grip. If yes, act thoughtfully.

**Practice for today:** Before responding to anything that irritates you, take one breath and ask, *"Is this mine to carry?"*

## B. AA Daily Reflection — "Deep Down Within Us"

**Title:** DEEP DOWN WITHIN US

The June 17 reflection centres on the idea that the Great Reality — the awareness of a Higher Power — is found not in the noise outside, but deep within ourselves. The reflection draws from the AA Big Book (p. 55): *search diligently within yourself... with this attitude you cannot fail.*

**Paraphrase:** Recovery is often thought of as a journey outward — to meetings, to sponsors, to service. But this reflection reminds us that the foundation of it all is interior. When we slow down enough to listen — in meetings, in quiet, in the daily work of the Steps — something becomes available to us that was always there: a sense of being held, heard, and not alone. The consciousness of belief doesn't usually arrive as a lightning bolt. It grows quietly, through showing up, through honesty, through willingness. The guarantee is gentle: *with this attitude, you cannot fail.*

## C. Bible Verse of the Day — Psalm 34:6 (NIV)

> *"This poor man called, and the Lord heard him; he saved him out of all his troubles."*
> — Psalm 34:6

**Context:** Psalm 34 is attributed to David, written during a moment of desperation — when he feigned madness before King Achish of Gath to escape death. The psalm is a celebration not of strength or status, but of *crying out*. The word translated "poor" (ani) in Hebrew carries the sense of being lowly, humble, afflicted — without resources to save oneself. What the psalmist discovered: the act of turning to God *is* the miracle. The hearing precedes the saving. The call was enough.

**Reflection:** On days that feel hard, when you feel inadequate or overwhelmed, remember that the entry point isn't confidence — it's honesty. Just cry out.

## D. Merriam-Webster Word of the Day — **Gamut** *(noun)*

**Pronunciation:** GAM-ut

**Definition:** A complete range or series of related things. *"Runs the gamut"* means to cover the entire span from one extreme to the other.

**Example sentence:** *Her emotions ran the gamut from nervous anticipation to quiet joy as the day unfolded.*

**Etymology:** This word traces back to medieval music theory. An 11th-century Italian monk and music theorist named Guido d'Arezzo invented a system for naming musical tones using syllables — the forerunner of do-re-mi. The lowest note on his scale was called *gamma ut* (gamma being the Greek letter used as a musical symbol, and *ut* being the first syllable of his scale). Over time, *gamma ut* collapsed into *gamut*, and came to mean the full musical scale — and eventually, any complete range of anything.

## E. This Day in History — June 17

**1631 — The Death that Built the Taj Mahal**
Mumtaz Mahal, beloved wife of Mughal Emperor Shah Jahan, died in childbirth. Her loss so devastated the emperor that he spent the next 22 years constructing the Taj Mahal as her tomb. What we know as one of the world's most beautiful buildings was born entirely from grief.

**1775 — Battle of Bunker Hill**
British General Thomas Gage led his forces against American colonists on Breed's Hill near Boston. Though the British won the ground, they suffered staggering casualties. The battle showed the colonists they could stand and fight — and convinced the British this would not be a quick war.

**1885 — The Statue of Liberty Arrives in New York**
France's gift arrived in 214 crates aboard the French frigate Isère — disassembled. It would take over a year to reassemble and dedicate. The moment was a reminder that great things often arrive in pieces before they become whole.

**1928 — Amelia Earhart Crosses the Atlantic**
Earhart became the first woman to fly across the Atlantic Ocean, though she served as a passenger — the real flying done by pilot Wilmer Stultz. She was characteristically self-deprecating about it, calling herself "just a sack of potatoes." But she went on to pilot the crossing solo in 1932. The world remembered the name, not the qualifier.

**1944 — Iceland Becomes a Republic**
Having been under Danish rule for centuries, Iceland formally established itself as an independent republic. The Nordic island nation — population roughly 130,000 at the time — quietly became one of the world's most stable democracies.

**1972 — The Watergate Break-In**
Five men were arrested inside the Democratic National Committee headquarters at the Watergate complex in Washington, D.C. A seemingly minor burglary set off a chain of events that would end with President Nixon's resignation in 1974 — the only presidential resignation in U.S. history.

## F. Britannica Fact

The entire musical scale — *do, re, mi, fa, sol, la, ti* — was invented by a Benedictine monk. Guido d'Arezzo (c. 991–1033 AD) created the system of solfège so that choristers could learn new hymns without having to hear them first. He named each syllable after the opening syllable of successive lines of a Latin hymn to St. John the Baptist (*Ut queant laxis*). The original first note, *ut*, was later replaced with *do* because it was easier to sing. Guido also invented the four-line musical staff — the direct ancestor of the five-line staff still used today. One medieval monk changed everything about how humans read, teach, and remember music.
