Module 01
Legal Framework for OHS in Construction
⏱ 15 min session 📖 Read → 🧠 Recall → ✏️ Apply
Legislative Hierarchy — Act > Regulation > Code
Construction safety in Alberta sits on three layers. Think of them as a pyramid: the OHS Act is the law passed by the Legislature — it sets out rights, duties, and enforcement powers. The OHS Regulation fills in administrative details (forms, procedures, committee requirements). The OHS Code is the technical rulebook — it specifies exactly how you must control specific hazards (fall protection heights, scaffolding, confined space procedures). If there's a conflict, the Act wins.
  • OHS Act: creates the Internal Responsibility System (IRS). Everyone — from owner to new worker — shares responsibility. No one can contract out of OHS duties.
  • OHS Regulation: sets administrative requirements like Joint Work Site Health and Safety Committee (JWSHSC) composition and meeting frequency.
  • OHS Code: 41 parts with specific technical standards. E.g., Part 9 = Fall Protection, Part 40 = Confined Spaces.
Prime Contractor
Required on any worksite with two or more employers working at the same time. The Prime Contractor is designated in writing and is responsible for coordinating all OHS activities on site. On a construction project this is usually the general contractor. If no Prime Contractor is designated, every employer at the site is jointly responsible.
  • Must have a safety program that covers all workers on site — including subcontractors.
  • Can be held liable for OHS violations even if the violation was committed by a sub's worker.
Key Roles and Duties
  • Owner: ensures the worksite is safe to work at; must inform employers of known hazards.
  • Employer: hazard identification, training, supervision, PPE provision, incident investigation. Must ensure workers are competent (trained, experienced, and qualified).
  • Supervisor: must be competent; must know the OHS rules for the work they supervise; must ensure workers follow rules.
  • Worker: work safely; use PPE; report hazards; participate in training.
Three Worker Rights (know these cold)
  • Right to Know: workers must be informed of all hazards. Includes WHMIS training, site orientation, FLHA results.
  • Right to Participate: workers can join the Joint Work Site Health and Safety Committee (JWSHSC). Required when 20+ workers are at a worksite for 90+ days.
  • Right to Refuse: a worker can refuse work they believe presents an imminent danger to themselves or others. The worker reports to the supervisor → supervisor investigates → if unresolved, an OHS officer is called. The worker cannot be penalized for a legitimate refusal.
Due Diligence — Your Legal Defence
An employer charged with an OHS violation can defend themselves by proving they exercised due diligence — meaning they took every reasonable precaution. This requires: documented hazard assessments, written procedures, training records, supervision, and timely corrective actions. "I didn't know" is not due diligence. Documentation is everything. Enforcement Mechanisms
  • Inspection: OHS officers can enter any worksite without notice, inspect anything, interview anyone.
  • Order: officer can issue compliance orders with deadlines.
  • Stop-Work Order: immediate work stoppage when imminent danger exists. Work cannot resume until hazard is corrected and officer lifts the order.
  • Prosecution: fines up to $500,000 per violation per day in Alberta. Directors and officers of corporations can be personally charged. Repeat offenders face larger penalties.
Mnemonic: "PERL" for worker rights — Participate, know (E as in Education), Refuse, Lives saved

Module 02
Health & Safety Program Basics
⏱ 15 min session 📖 Read → 🧠 Recall → ✏️ Apply
Nine Core Elements of a Construction H&S Program
Mnemonic: "Please Remember Hazards Train Inspectors In Emergency Records Promptly" (Policy, Roles, Hazard ID, Training, Inspections, Incident investigation, Emergency response, Records, Program review)
  • 1. Policy: a written statement of the employer's commitment to safety. Must be signed by the most senior person (CEO or president) — not just the safety manager. The signature signals that safety is a company value, not just a department function. Must be posted, reviewed annually, and communicated to all workers.
  • 2. Roles & Responsibilities: every person's OHS duties are defined in writing — from owner down to worker. Prevents the "that's not my job" defence. Supervisors must understand they are legally accountable.
  • 3. Hazard Identification & Control: formal systems to find and control hazards before injuries occur. Includes FLHA (task-level), JHA (job-level), inspections (site-level), and incident investigation (post-event).
  • 4. Training & Competency: workers must be trained before performing a task. Training must be documented (date, content, trainer, worker signature). Competency = trained + experienced + capable. New worker orientation is mandatory on first day — covers emergency procedures, hazards, rights, reporting.
  • 5. Inspections: both scheduled (monthly safety walks) and unannounced. Use a checklist. Document findings. Assign corrective actions with deadlines and responsible persons. Close out all findings.
  • 6. Incident Investigation: all incidents must be investigated — near misses, first-aid cases, medical-aid cases, lost-time injuries, and fatalities. Serious injuries/fatalities must be reported to OHS within 48 hours. Root cause analysis, not blame assignment. Corrective actions prevent recurrence.
  • 7. Emergency Response: written plan covering fire, spill, injury, severe weather. Includes: assembly points, first-aid locations, emergency contacts, evacuation procedures, communication plan. Drills are required — document them.
  • 8. Records & Statistics: maintain FLHA logs, training records, inspection reports, incident investigation reports, meeting minutes, equipment inspection records. Calculate injury rates (TRIR, LTIR) to track program performance.
  • 9. Program Review: evaluate the entire program at least annually. Use leading and lagging indicators. Compare against industry benchmarks. Update after any serious incident.
FLHA vs. JHA — Know the Difference
  • FLHA (Field-Level Hazard Assessment): done by the crew at the actual work location before each shift or when conditions change. Takes 5–15 minutes. Worker-driven. All workers on the task must sign it. If a new hazard appears mid-task, stop work and redo the FLHA.
  • JHA / JSA (Job Hazard Analysis / Job Safety Analysis): a detailed written document prepared in advance (often in the office), breaking a job into sequential steps and identifying hazards and controls for each step. More thorough than FLHA. Used for high-hazard tasks or new work processes. Supervisor typically leads it.
What Makes a "Competent Supervisor"?
Under Alberta OHS legislation, a competent supervisor must: (1) know the OHS regulations that apply to the work, (2) be experienced in the type of work being supervised, and (3) be qualified to supervise safely. Simply having a supervisor title is not enough — they need demonstrated knowledge and experience. Safety officers often test this in audits. Leading vs. Lagging Indicators
  • Lagging indicators: measure past failures. Examples: lost-time injury rate, TRIR (total recordable injury rate), number of fatalities. They tell you what already went wrong.
  • Leading indicators: measure proactive safety activities. Examples: number of FLHAs completed, inspection completion rate, near-miss reports submitted, % of workers with current training. They predict future performance.
A strong safety program uses BOTH — leading indicators to prevent incidents, lagging indicators to measure outcome trends.

Module 06
Hazard Identification Methods
⏱ 15 min session 📖 Read → 🧠 Recall → ✏️ Apply
Hazard vs. Risk — The Foundational Distinction
A hazard is anything with the potential to cause harm (e.g., an unguarded floor opening). A risk is the likelihood and severity of harm actually occurring from that hazard. Hazard identification finds hazards. Risk assessment evaluates them. You cannot control what you haven't identified. Risk Matrix: Likelihood × Consequence = Risk Level
Rate likelihood (1–5: rare → almost certain) and consequence (1–5: negligible → catastrophic). Multiply them. High scores (15–25) require immediate controls. Medium (8–12) require planned controls. Low (1–6) = acceptable with monitoring. Always document your matrix ratings. FLHA — Field-Level Hazard Assessment
  • When: before each shift and whenever site conditions change (weather, new equipment, new crew, task change).
  • Who: every worker who will perform the task. The crew — not just the supervisor — walks the work area, identifies hazards, and signs the FLHA form. All signatures are required before work begins.
  • What to do mid-task: if a new hazard is identified (e.g., a gas line is unexpectedly hit), STOP WORK immediately, reassess the hazard, implement new controls, update the FLHA, re-sign, then resume. Never work around an unaddressed new hazard.
  • Documentation: FLHAs must be retained on site and available for OHS officer review. They are legal documents and evidence of due diligence.
JHA — Job Hazard Analysis (Step-by-Step Process)
  1. Select the job to analyze (high-hazard, new, or after an incident)
  2. Break the job into sequential steps (typically 6–15 steps)
  3. Identify hazards at each step — ask "what could go wrong here?"
  4. Apply the hierarchy of controls to each identified hazard
  5. Document and communicate the JHA to all workers on the task
  6. Review and update after any incident or change to the job
Five Hazard Identification Methods
  • Formal inspections: scheduled walkthroughs using checklists. Unannounced inspections catch real conditions. Document every finding, assign corrective actions, and verify completion.
  • FLHA: task-level, field-based, before each shift. (See above.)
  • JHA/JSA: job-level, step-by-step, prepared in advance. (See above.)
  • Near-miss reporting: a near miss is an unplanned event with NO injury but the potential for one. Heinrich's Triangle: for every fatality there are ~30 serious injuries, ~300 minor injuries, and ~3,000 near misses. Reporting near misses prevents future fatalities.
  • Incident trend analysis: track types, times, locations, and trades of incidents over months. If 60% of hand injuries occur on Mondays during concrete forming, you have actionable intelligence. Target that crew, that task, that day.
Hierarchy of Controls Connection
Hazard identification feeds directly into the hierarchy of controls. You can't select the right control level without knowing the hazard type, likelihood, and consequence. The risk matrix determines how urgently you need to act and at what control level. Memory Aid: "FIJNT" — FLHA, Inspections, JHA, Near-miss, Trend analysis

Module 08
Hierarchy of Controls
⏱ 15 min session 📖 Read → 🧠 Recall → ✏️ Apply
The Hierarchy — Most to Least Effective
Mnemonic: "Every Safety Engineer Adores PPE" (Elimination, Substitution, Engineering, Administrative, PPE). The higher up you go, the more permanent and reliable the protection — because it doesn't depend on human behaviour.
  • 1. Elimination: physically remove the hazard so it no longer exists. The most effective because there is nothing left to cause harm. Examples: (a) pre-fabricate components at ground level so workers don't need to work at height; (b) use pre-cut lumber to eliminate table saw hazard; (c) schedule underground utility work before surface traffic begins to eliminate struck-by risk. Ask: "Can we redesign the job so this hazard doesn't exist?"
  • 2. Substitution: replace a hazardous material or process with a less hazardous one. Examples: (a) water-based paint instead of solvent-based paint (reduces VOC exposure); (b) battery-powered tools instead of gas-powered in confined spaces (eliminates CO hazard); (c) lighter precast panels instead of poured-in-place to reduce manual handling injury. Key: the substitute must be genuinely less hazardous — not just different.
  • 3. Engineering Controls: physical modifications to the worksite or equipment that create a barrier between the worker and the hazard. Examples: (a) guardrails and toe boards at floor openings (fall hazard); (b) local exhaust ventilation in spray painting booth (chemical hazard); (c) machine guards on rotating equipment (caught-in hazard); (d) concrete barriers between traffic and workers (struck-by hazard). Engineering controls are passive — they work even if the worker forgets. Highly reliable.
  • 4. Administrative Controls: change HOW work is done through procedures, training, scheduling, and permits. Examples: (a) hot work permit system (fire hazard); (b) rotate workers on vibrating equipment to limit exposure time (vibration hazard); (c) two-person rule for confined space entry; (d) training and safe work procedures for chemical handling; (e) signage and barricades directing traffic. Administrative controls DEPEND on human compliance — less reliable than engineering. Must be enforced.
  • 5. PPE (Personal Protective Equipment): equipment worn by the worker to reduce exposure to residual hazard AFTER all other controls have been applied. Examples: (a) full-body harness + lanyard (fall arrest, last line after guardrails fail); (b) hard hat (falling objects); (c) N95 respirator (silica dust after ventilation is in place); (d) cut-resistant gloves (hand lacerations); (e) hearing protection (noise above 85 dB after engineering controls). PPE fails due to: improper fit, inadequate training, non-compliance, damage/wear, wrong type selected.
Why PPE Fails
PPE is worker-dependent. It fails when: (1) the wrong type is selected, (2) it doesn't fit properly, (3) the worker removes it momentarily, (4) it is damaged and not replaced, (5) the worker is not trained on correct use and donning/doffing. A guardrail never calls in sick. PPE does. Residual Risk and Defence in Depth
  • Residual risk: the risk that remains AFTER controls are applied. No control eliminates risk to zero. Residual risk is documented and accepted (or triggers additional layers).
  • Defence in depth: applying multiple control layers simultaneously for high-risk tasks. Example for fall hazard: elimination (work at grade) + engineering (guardrail) + administrative (rescue plan) + PPE (harness). If one layer fails, the next catches the hazard. Construction sites use this because single controls are rarely enough for the Fatal Four.
Documenting Control Selection
Your JHA or FLHA should document: (1) the hazard identified, (2) which control level was selected, (3) WHY a higher-level control was not feasible (if applicable), and (4) residual risk after controls. Auditors look for documented justification — "we couldn't eliminate it because the task requires height work" is acceptable if written.

Module 15
WHMIS & Safety Data Sheets (SDS)
⏱ 15 min session 📖 Read → 🧠 Recall → ✏️ Apply
WHMIS 2015 — The Big Picture
WHMIS 2015 aligns Canada with the Globally Harmonized System (GHS) — the international standard for chemical hazard communication. Every hazardous product used in a workplace must have a compliant label and an SDS. Workers must be trained before using any hazardous product. Training must be documented. The 6 Required Label Elements
  1. Product identifier (the product name)
  2. Pictogram(s) (hazard symbols in red diamond border)
  3. Signal word (DANGER or WARNING)
  4. Hazard statement(s) (describes the nature of the hazard: e.g., "Causes serious eye damage")
  5. Precautionary statement(s) (what to do: e.g., "Wear eye protection, wash hands after use")
  6. Supplier information (name and address of Canadian supplier)
Signal Words: DANGER = more severe hazard category. WARNING = less severe. Both require attention but DANGER products need more rigorous controls. All 9 WHMIS Pictograms
  • 🔥 Flame: flammable gases, liquids, solids, aerosols, self-reactives, pyrophorics
  • 🔥⭕ Flame over circle: oxidizing gases, liquids, solids — can cause or intensify fire
  • 💥 Exploding bomb: explosives, self-reactives, organic peroxides
  • 💀 Skull and crossbones: acute toxicity (fatal or toxic if swallowed/inhaled/skin contact)
  • ❗ Exclamation mark: less severe health hazards — skin/eye irritation, skin sensitizer, narcotic effects
  • 🫁 Health hazard (torso with starburst): carcinogen, reproductive toxin, respiratory sensitizer, STOT, aspiration hazard
  • 🧪 Corrosion: skin corrosion, eye damage, corrosive to metals
  • 🫧 Gas cylinder: gases under pressure (compressed, liquefied, dissolved, refrigerated liquefied)
  • 🌿 Environment: aquatic toxicity (acute or chronic) — voluntary in Canada but used by many suppliers
Biohazardous materials use a separate biohazard symbol (three interlocking circles) and are regulated under both WHMIS and federal biological safety standards. All 16 SDS Sections — What's in Each
  • 1. Identification: product name, use, supplier contact, emergency phone
  • 2. Hazard identification: classification, label elements, other hazards
  • 3. Composition: ingredients and CAS numbers
  • 4. First-aid measures: by exposure route (inhalation, skin, eye, ingestion)
  • 5. Fire-fighting measures: extinguisher types, explosion hazards
  • 6. Accidental release measures: spill response, containment, clean-up procedures
  • 7. Handling and storage: safe use practices, storage temperature, incompatibles
  • 8. Exposure controls / PPE: OELs/TLVs, engineering controls, respiratory/hand/eye protection required
  • 9. Physical and chemical properties: flash point, boiling point, odour, pH, vapour pressure
  • 10. Stability and reactivity: conditions to avoid, incompatible materials
  • 11. Toxicological information: LD50, exposure effects by route, carcinogenicity
  • 12. Ecological information: environmental fate, aquatic toxicity
  • 13. Disposal considerations: waste disposal methods, regulatory requirements
  • 14. Transport information: UN number, shipping name, packing group (TDG)
  • 15. Regulatory information: other regulations that apply
  • 16. Other information: revision date, changes from previous version
Memory shortcut for exam-tested sections: "4 helps you survive it, 6 cleans it up, 8 protects you from it, 14 moves it." Exposure Limits
  • OEL (Occupational Exposure Limit): Alberta's legal limit for airborne exposure. Set in OHS Code.
  • TLV (Threshold Limit Value): ACGIH recommended values, often referenced in SDS Section 8. Not legally binding but widely used.
  • TWA (Time-Weighted Average): average over 8-hour shift. STEL (Short-Term Exposure Limit): 15-minute peak limit.
Spill Response: Consult SDS Section 6. General steps: (1) alert others/evacuate area, (2) don appropriate PPE per Section 8, (3) contain spill (absorbents, berms), (4) prevent entry to drains/waterways, (5) clean up per instructions, (6) dispose per Section 13, (7) report per environmental regulations. Training Documentation Requirements
Employers must document: (1) which products workers are trained on, (2) date of training, (3) trainer name, (4) worker signature. Training must be renewed when a new hazardous product is introduced or when the SDS is significantly revised.

5. Section 4 (First-Aid Measures) — broken down by exposure route: skin, eyes, inhalation, ingestion.
6. OEL = Alberta's legally enforceable limit (OHS Code). TLV = ACGIH recommendation, widely referenced but not legally binding. Use OEL for compliance; use TLV as a best-practice guide.
7. Flame; Flame over circle (oxidizer); Exploding bomb; Skull & crossbones; Exclamation mark; Health hazard (torso); Corrosion; Gas cylinder; Environment.
8. Alert/evacuate; don PPE per SDS Section 8; contain spill; prevent drain entry; clean up per Section 6; dispose per Section 13; report to supervisor and environmental authorities if required.
9. Obtain the SDS. Verify it is WHMIS 2015 compliant (has all 16 sections). Ensure the container is properly labelled. Train any worker who will use or work near the product before use begins. Document the training.
10. Products covered; date of training; trainer's name; worker signature. Must be updated when a new product is introduced or SDS is revised.

Construction Hazards: Falls, Struck-By, Electrical, Confined Space
⏱ 15 min session 📖 Read → 🧠 Recall → ✏️ Apply
The Fatal Four — #1 Killers in Construction
Falls, Struck-By, Caught-In/Between, and Electrocution account for over 60% of construction fatalities. Know them, own them. 1. Falls — #1 Cause of Construction Fatalities
Fall protection is mandatory in Alberta when workers are exposed to a fall of 3 metres (10 ft) or more for most construction tasks. Some tasks (e.g., steel erection, roofing) have lower thresholds.
  • Travel restraint: prevents the worker from reaching the fall edge. Lanyard length limits how far the worker can travel. Simplest form — but requires a proper anchor point.
  • Fall arrest: allows movement to the edge but stops the fall after it begins. Requires: rated anchor point (min. 22 kN), full-body harness, shock-absorbing lanyard or PFAS. The worker WILL fall some distance — clearance below must be calculated (fall distance + deceleration + safety margin).
  • Safety nets: passive system — catches a falling worker. Must be installed within 6 m below the work surface. Inspected before each shift.
  • Guardrails: engineering control. Top rail at 1.07 m (42 in), mid-rail, toe board. Most preferred after elimination.
  • Fall protection plan: required when conventional fall protection (guardrails, PFAS) is not practicable. Must be site-specific and signed by a competent person.
  • Scaffolding: must be erected, altered, and dismantled under direct supervision of a competent person. Inspected before each use. Rated for intended load. Guardrails on all open sides above 3 m.
2. Struck-By
Falling objects, swinging crane loads, reversing equipment, and projectiles. Controls: hard hats (CSA Z94.1), tool tethers at height, exclusion zones under crane picks, spotters for reversing equipment, orange vests (hi-vis) near traffic, overhead protection (catch platforms). Barricade a radius equal to the height of work above. 3. Caught-In / Between — LOTO (Lockout/Tagout)
LOTO prevents the unexpected release of stored energy (electrical, mechanical, hydraulic, pneumatic, gravitational, thermal, chemical). The 8-step LOTO procedure:
  1. Notify affected workers
  2. Identify all energy sources
  3. Shut down the equipment using normal stopping procedure
  4. Isolate energy sources (disconnect switches, valves closed)
  5. Apply locks and tags — each worker applies their OWN lock
  6. Release / restrain stored energy (bleed pressure, block gravity)
  7. Verify zero energy state (try to start — press start button)
  8. Perform work safely
Re-energize in reverse order. Never remove another worker's lock. Trenching and Excavation — Soil Classification
  • Type A soil: cohesive, stable (stiff clay). Max slope for unshored trench: 3:4 (53°). Lowest risk.
  • Type B soil: medium stability (angular gravel, previously disturbed soil). Max slope: 1:1 (45°).
  • Type C soil: least stable (granular, submerged, cracked). Max slope: 1½:1 (34°). Highest risk — shoring or shielding often required.
Trenches deeper than 1.5 m require a safety system (sloping, shoring, or shielding). Inspect after rain, frost, or vibration events. 4. Electrical Hazards
  • Overhead power lines: minimum setback in Alberta — 7 metres for lines up to 750 V; larger setbacks for higher voltages. Contact the utility owner before working within the limit of approach. Never assume a line is de-energized.
  • Underground utilities: call Alberta 1-Call (Dig Safe) at least 3 days before digging. Wait for locates. Hand-dig within 1 m of locate marks.
  • Temporary wiring: use GFCIs (Ground Fault Circuit Interrupters) on all temporary power on construction sites. Inspect cords daily — no damaged insulation. Extension cords are not permanent wiring.
5. Confined Space
A confined space: large enough to enter, not designed for continuous occupancy, limited entry/exit. A permit-required confined space has one or more: hazardous atmosphere, engulfment hazard, internal configuration hazard, or other serious safety/health hazard.
  • Atmospheric testing order: (1) Oxygen first (19.5–23% = acceptable range), then (2) flammable/explosive gases (<10% LEL acceptable), then (3) toxic gases (CO, H₂S, etc. below OEL). Test BEFORE entry and continuously during work.
  • 5 requirements for permit-required CS entry: (1) entry permit issued; (2) atmospheric testing complete; (3) isolation from hazardous energy; (4) trained attendant at entry point at all times; (5) rescue plan in place and rescue team on standby.
  • Hot work permits: required for welding, cutting, grinding near flammable materials. Includes: fire watch during work AND 30–60 minutes after. Fire extinguisher on hand. Remove or protect combustibles within 10 m.

Domain 1
People — Leading & Managing Project Teams
⏱ 15 min session 📖 Read → 🧠 Recall → ✏️ Apply
Tuckman's 5 Team Development Stages
Mnemonic: "Forming Storms the Norm — Perform, then Adjourn"
  • Forming: team meets, roles unclear, everyone polite. PM role: set direction, establish ground rules, clarify goals and expectations.
  • Storming: conflict emerges — personality clashes, disagreements on approach, competition for roles. Normal and necessary. PM role: facilitate conflict resolution, coach through disagreements, don't suppress conflict.
  • Norming: team establishes norms, trust builds, collaboration improves. Roles clarify. PM role: step back, encourage ownership, celebrate progress.
  • Performing: team is high-functioning, self-directed, interdependent. PM role: remove obstacles (servant leadership), delegate, shield team from distractions.
  • Adjourning: project ends, team disbands. PM role: celebrate wins, conduct lessons learned, support team transitions. Can trigger grief-like responses.
Conflict Resolution — Thomas-Kilmann Model
Ranked most to least preferred on the PMP exam:
  1. Collaborate / Problem-solve: both parties work together to find a win-win solution. Best long-term outcome. Time-intensive. PMP exam almost always prefers this.
  2. Compromise / Reconcile: both give something up. Useful when time is limited and both parties have valid needs. "Lose-lose light."
  3. Accommodate / Smooth: one party yields to keep the peace. Preserves relationship short-term but may breed resentment. Use when you're wrong or the issue is minor.
  4. Avoid / Withdraw: postpone or sidestep the issue. Useful only when the issue will resolve itself or you need time to cool down. Does not resolve anything.
  5. Force / Direct: one party imposes their will. Fastest but damages relationships. Use only in emergencies or when you have authority and time is critical.
RACI Matrix
  • R — Responsible: does the work.
  • A — Accountable: owns the outcome; signs off. Only ONE person per task.
  • C — Consulted: provides input; two-way communication.
  • I — Informed: receives updates; one-way communication.
Use RACI to prevent confusion on who decides, who does, and who just needs to know. Motivation Theories
  • Maslow's Hierarchy: physiological → safety → social → esteem → self-actualization. People are motivated by their unmet needs.
  • Herzberg's Two-Factor: hygiene factors (salary, job security, work conditions) prevent dissatisfaction but don't motivate. Motivators (achievement, recognition, growth) drive engagement.
  • McGregor's Theory X: workers are lazy, need control and threats. Theory Y: workers are self-motivated, seek responsibility. Modern PMs use Theory Y.
  • McClelland's Needs: achievement (drives to excel), affiliation (needs relationships), power (wants influence). Tailor your approach to the team member's dominant need.
Virtual Team Challenges and Solutions
Challenges: time zones, cultural differences, communication gaps, isolation, accountability. Solutions: clear communication plan, regular video check-ins, shared collaboration tools (Jira, Confluence, Teams), overlap hours for real-time communication, explicit norms documented in team charter. 360-Degree Feedback vs. Coaching vs. Mentoring
  • 360-degree feedback: performance input collected from peers, direct reports, supervisors, and self. Provides a full picture — reduces blind spots.
  • Coaching: PM helps team member improve a specific skill or behaviour through guided questions. Short-term, performance-focused.
  • Mentoring: experienced person provides long-term guidance and career development. Relationship-based, broader scope than coaching.
Emotional Intelligence (EI): self-awareness (know your triggers), self-regulation (manage your reactions), motivation (internal drive), empathy (understand others' feelings), social skills (build relationships). High EI = more effective conflict resolution and stakeholder management. Resource Management Plan: documents how team members will be acquired, managed, and released. Includes: roles and responsibilities, team charter, training plans, rewards and recognition system. Created during planning, updated throughout.

Domain 2
Process — Managing Project Work
⏱ 15 min session 📖 Read → 🧠 Recall → ✏️ Apply
Project Lifecycle and Planning
  • Project charter: authorizes the project and formally names the PM. Issued by the sponsor. Contains: project purpose, high-level scope, budget, timeline, key stakeholders, PM authority level. Without a charter, the PM has no formal authority.
  • Rolling wave planning: near-term work is planned in detail; future work is planned at a high level and refined as more information becomes available. Used in both predictive and agile approaches. Accounts for uncertainty.
  • WBS (Work Breakdown Structure): hierarchical decomposition of project scope into deliverables and work packages. The 100% rule: the WBS must capture 100% of project scope — nothing inside, nothing outside. Work packages are the lowest level — small enough to estimate and assign. The WBS Dictionary documents each element: description, owner, acceptance criteria, resource requirements, estimated cost/duration.
Network Diagram and Critical Path
  • AON (Activity on Node): boxes represent activities, arrows represent dependencies. PMP exam standard.
  • Critical Path: the longest path through the network from start to finish. It determines the minimum project duration. Zero float on all critical path activities.
  • Float (Slack): how long a non-critical activity can be delayed without delaying the project. Total float = LS − ES or LF − EF. Free float = how long an activity can delay without delaying the next activity.
  • Crashing: add resources to critical path activities to shorten duration. Increases cost. Best to crash the cheapest activities first.
  • Fast-tracking: overlap activities that were planned sequentially. Increases risk. No cost increase. Use when schedule is tight and activities can partially overlap.
3-Point Estimating (PERT)
  • Formula: Expected Duration = (Optimistic + 4 × Most Likely + Pessimistic) ÷ 6
  • Example: O=4 days, M=6 days, P=14 days → E = (4 + 24 + 14) / 6 = 7 days
  • Standard deviation = (P − O) / 6. Used in Monte Carlo simulation to model schedule risk.
EVM Formulas — Know All of These
  • SV (Schedule Variance) = EV − PV. Negative = behind schedule.
  • CV (Cost Variance) = EV − AC. Negative = over budget.
  • SPI (Schedule Performance Index) = EV / PV. <1 = behind.
  • CPI (Cost Performance Index) = EV / AC. <1 = over budget.
  • EAC (Estimate at Completion) = BAC / CPI. Forecasted total cost if current CPI continues.
  • ETC (Estimate to Complete) = EAC − AC. How much more you'll spend.
  • TCPI (To-Complete Performance Index) = (BAC − EV) / (BAC − AC). The CPI you must achieve on remaining work to hit the original budget. >1 = harder than current performance.
  • VAC (Variance at Completion) = BAC − EAC. Projected surplus or deficit at project end.
Memory trick: "EV comes first in every formula — it's what you actually earned." Risk Management
  • Risk register: living document containing: risk description, probability, impact, risk score, owner, response strategy, trigger, status. Updated throughout the project.
  • Issue log: tracks problems that have ALREADY occurred (risks that materialized). Risk register = potential future events. Issue log = current problems.
  • Risk probability/impact matrix: plot each risk on a grid (probability on one axis, impact on the other). High-probability + high-impact risks get active responses. Low-low risks are accepted/monitored.
  • Threat responses: Avoid (eliminate the risk), Transfer (shift impact to third party — insurance, contracts), Mitigate (reduce probability or impact), Accept (acknowledge and monitor; passive = do nothing, active = set aside contingency reserve).
  • Opportunity responses: Exploit (make it happen), Share (partner with someone who can capture it), Enhance (increase probability/impact), Accept.
  • Monte Carlo simulation: runs thousands of scenarios using probability distributions for duration/cost estimates. Outputs a probability curve — e.g., "80% chance project completes within 120 days."
Lessons Learned: captured throughout the project (not just at close). Added to the organizational process assets repository. Used by future projects. Include: what worked, what didn't, root causes, recommendations.

Domain 3
Business Environment — Strategy & Governance
⏱ 15 min session 📖 Read → 🧠 Recall → ✏️ Apply
Portfolio / Program / Project Hierarchy
  • Portfolio: a collection of programs, projects, and operations managed together to achieve strategic objectives. Highest level. Prioritizes investment.
  • Program: a group of related projects managed in a coordinated way to obtain benefits not available from managing them individually. E.g., a highway expansion program with multiple construction projects.
  • Project: a temporary endeavour to create a unique product, service, or result. Defined start and end. A project is NOT ongoing operations.
Project Selection Tools
  • NPV (Net Present Value): present value of future cash inflows minus the initial investment. Higher NPV = better investment. Select the project with the highest NPV. If NPV < 0, the project destroys value.
  • ROI (Return on Investment): (Net Benefit / Cost) × 100. Higher % = better return. Simple but doesn't account for time value of money.
  • Payback Period: how long until the project recoups its investment. Shorter = better. Ignores cash flows after payback point and time value of money.
  • IRR (Internal Rate of Return): the discount rate that makes NPV = 0. If IRR > cost of capital, the project is worthwhile. Higher IRR = better.
  • Exam tip: when comparing projects, if one metric conflicts with another, NPV is generally considered the most reliable.
Benefits Management Plan
Documents: what benefits the project will deliver, how they will be measured, who is responsible for realizing them, and the timeline for realization. Benefits often extend BEYOND the project close date — the PM's job is to set up the measurement system, but operations/sponsors own realization after closeout. Organizational Change Management — ADKAR Model
When a project delivers change to an organization, people must move through:
  • A — Awareness: of the need for change
  • D — Desire: to support and participate in the change
  • K — Knowledge: of how to change
  • A — Ability: to implement the change (skills, tools)
  • R — Reinforcement: to sustain the change over time
If people are stuck at any ADKAR stage, address THAT stage — don't jump to training (K) if they don't yet have Desire (D). Organizational Structures
  • Functional: organized by department (IT, Finance, Engineering). PM has little to no authority. Resources are "borrowed" from functional managers.
  • Matrix (Weak/Balanced/Strong): PM shares authority with functional managers. Weak = FM dominates; Strong = PM dominates. Most common in large organizations.
  • Projectized: organized around projects. PM has full authority over resources. Team members are dedicated to projects. Best PM authority — but resources are released when the project ends.
PMO Types
  • Supportive: provides templates, training, best practices. Low control. Consultative role.
  • Controlling: requires compliance with frameworks, tools, and governance. Moderate control.
  • Directive: directly manages projects; assigns PMs. High control. PM reports to the PMO.
EEF vs. OPA
  • Enterprise Environmental Factors (EEF): external/internal conditions that influence but are NOT controlled by the project team. Examples: regulatory environment, market conditions, organizational culture, available technology, political climate.
  • Organizational Process Assets (OPA): plans, processes, policies, procedures, and knowledge bases owned by the organization. Examples: templates, lessons learned repository, historical data, process guidelines.
Stage Gate Reviews: formal reviews at the end of each project phase. Stakeholders evaluate progress against the business case. Possible outcomes: continue, modify, pause, or kill the project. Also called phase gate, kill point, or toll gate review. PMI Code of Ethics — 4 Values: Responsibility, Respect, Fairness, Honesty. Each has mandatory standards (must do) and aspirational standards (strive to do). Violations of mandatory standards can result in PMI disciplinary action.

The Agile Manifesto — 4 Values and 12 Principles
The Manifesto values the items on the LEFT more than the right (both sides have value):
  • Individuals and interactions over processes and tools
  • Working software over comprehensive documentation
  • Customer collaboration over contract negotiation
  • Responding to change over following a plan
Key principles (exam favorites): deliver working software frequently (weeks, not months); welcome changing requirements even late in development; business people and developers work together daily; the best architecture emerges from self-organizing teams; reflect and adjust regularly (retrospectives); simplicity — maximize the work NOT done; face-to-face conversation is the most efficient communication. User Stories
Format: "As a [type of user], I want [goal], so that [reason/benefit]."
Example: "As a site supervisor, I want to log daily safety inspections from my phone, so that I don't have to carry paper forms."
  • Acceptance criteria: specific conditions that must be met for the story to be considered complete. Written from the user's perspective. Tested during Sprint Review.
  • Definition of Done (DoD): team-level checklist applied to EVERY story — e.g., coded, unit tested, peer reviewed, integrated, documentation updated. Ensures consistent quality. Reduces technical debt.
  • Technical debt: shortcuts or incomplete work that creates future rework. Accumulates when teams skip the DoD. Must be managed — track it in the backlog and address it in future sprints.
Scrum Framework
  • Product Owner: owns the Product Backlog; prioritizes work by business value; represents stakeholders; accepts or rejects completed work. One PO per team.
  • Scrum Master: servant leader; removes impediments; coaches team on Scrum; facilitates events; protects team from external interruptions. Does NOT manage people.
  • Development Team: self-organizing, cross-functional, 3–9 members; collectively owns delivery; no sub-teams or titles within the team.
  • Sprint: time-boxed iteration of 1–4 weeks. Scope is locked once Sprint starts. Delivers a potentially shippable increment.
  • Sprint burndown chart: tracks remaining work (story points or hours) over the sprint. Ideal line slopes down to zero. Above ideal = behind. Below ideal = ahead.
  • Release planning: determines which features will be delivered across multiple sprints to meet a release goal. Uses velocity to forecast how many sprints are needed.
Kanban
Visualize all work on a board (To Do → In Progress → Done). Set WIP limits per column — no column can exceed its limit. This forces finishing before starting new work, reveals bottlenecks, and improves flow. No fixed sprints — work flows continuously. Measure cycle time and throughput. Scaling Frameworks
  • SAFe (Scaled Agile Framework): organizes multiple agile teams into Agile Release Trains (ARTs). Works at portfolio, program, and team levels. Common in large enterprises.
  • LeSS (Large-Scale Scrum): applies Scrum principles to multiple teams working on one product. Simpler than SAFe. One Product Backlog, one Product Owner, multiple teams.
Hybrid Approaches
Combine predictive and agile based on project needs. Decision factors: regulatory requirements (predictive), requirements clarity (unclear → agile), team experience, stakeholder tolerance for ambiguity, contract type (fixed price favours predictive). Common pattern: predictive for planning and governance, agile for delivery sprints. Stakeholders engage more frequently in agile — sprint reviews replace quarterly status reports. Stakeholder Engagement in Agile: stakeholders attend Sprint Reviews to see working product and give feedback. This replaces long approval cycles. Product Owner is the daily stakeholder proxy on the team.

Practice Questions
1. Name the three Scrum roles and their primary responsibility.
2. What happens in a Sprint Retrospective? Who attends?
3. What is a WIP limit in Kanban? Why does limiting WIP improve flow?
4. You're managing a project with clear, fixed requirements and a compliance deadline. Agile or predictive? Why?
5. Write a user story for a construction safety officer who needs to log near-miss reports digitally.
6. What is the difference between acceptance criteria and the Definition of Done?
7. What are the 4 Agile Manifesto values? (Write them from memory.)
8. Your team's Sprint burndown chart is consistently above the ideal line for three sprints. What does this indicate and what do you do?
9. SCENARIO: A stakeholder calls you mid-sprint and says they need a new feature added immediately. What do you do?
10. What is technical debt? How does the Definition of Done help manage it?
Answer Key
Cover answers and write yours first.
1. Product Owner = owns backlog, prioritizes by value. Scrum Master = servant leader, removes impediments, coaches Scrum. Development Team = self-organizing, cross-functional, delivers the increment.
2. Retrospective = team reflects on HOW they worked (process, collaboration, tools) and identifies improvements for the next sprint. Entire Scrum team attends (PO, SM, Dev Team). Stakeholders do NOT attend.
3. WIP limit = maximum number of items allowed in a column at one time. Limiting WIP forces the team to finish work before starting new work, exposes bottlenecks, and reduces context-switching — all of which improve flow and reduce cycle time.
4. Predictive. Fixed requirements, compliance deadline, and defined deliverable all favour a predictive (waterfall) approach. The constraints are known upfront and the cost of change is high.
5. "As a safety officer, I want to submit near-miss reports from my mobile phone, so that I can document incidents immediately without returning to the site office."
6. Acceptance criteria = specific conditions for ONE user story to be complete (story-level). Definition of Done = team-wide checklist applied to ALL stories (team-level standard for quality).
7. (1) Individuals and interactions over processes and tools. (2) Working software over comprehensive documentation. (3) Customer collaboration over contract negotiation. (4) Responding to change over following a plan.
8. Team is consistently taking on more work than they can complete in a sprint (overcommitting) or there are impediments slowing them down. Actions: facilitate a retrospective to identify root cause; adjust sprint planning to use actual velocity; Scrum Master investigates and removes impediments.
9. Do not add to the current sprint — the sprint scope is locked. Direct the stakeholder to add the request to the Product Backlog. The Product Owner will prioritize it for a future sprint. Explain that this protects the team's ability to deliver on their current commitment.
10. Technical debt = shortcuts or incomplete work that will require future rework (e.g., skipping tests, hardcoding values). The DoD prevents it by requiring that every story meets quality standards before being called done — reducing the accumulation of debt over time.
🔗 Exam Connection: The PMP exam is approximately 50% agile/hybrid content. User stories, the Definition of Done, Sprint events, and stakeholder engagement in agile are heavily tested. The most common exam trap is choosing to add work mid-sprint — always direct new requests to the Product Backlog. Know the difference between Sprint Review (show working product to stakeholders) and Sprint Retrospective (internal team process improvement).
