⚖️ A · Marcus Aurelius / Daily Stoic Reflection
On the Power of the Mind
"You have power over your mind — not outside events. Realize this, and you will find strength."
— Marcus Aurelius, Meditations

What Marcus meant: Writing in the 2nd century AD as Emperor of Rome, Marcus constantly reminded himself that external circumstances — wars, politics, illness, other people's behaviour — were beyond his control. The only thing he could truly govern was his own mind and response. For him, this wasn't resignation; it was liberation.

Applied today: Today you have a delivery arriving, a task overdue, and a full evening of family. Some things will go sideways. What you can choose is how you meet each moment — with patience or frustration, presence or anxiety. That choice belongs entirely to you.

✨ Practice for today: When something doesn't go as planned, pause for one breath and ask: "Is this within my control?"
🌿 B · AA Daily Reflection — June 2
The Upward Path
"Recovery is not a destination — it is a continuous, one-day-at-a-time journey upward."

Today's AA Daily Reflection is titled The Upward Path. It reminds us that sobriety isn't simply the absence of drinking; it is the daily, active choice to live differently — with honesty, humility, and reliance on a Higher Power and the fellowship of others.

On the hard days, it can feel like walking uphill. But that's exactly what growth looks like. Each step forward — however small — is the path itself. Keep walking.

✝️ C · Bible Verse of the Day
Proverbs 28:13 — NIV
"Whoever conceals their sins does not prosper, but the one who confesses and renounces them finds mercy."

Context: The Hebrew word for "mercy" here is hesed — not just forgiveness, but loyal, covenant love. Concealment breeds shame and separation; confession opens the path to healing.

Today: This verse and the AA reflection sing the same song. Both point to the same ancient truth: honesty and letting go is where freedom begins. You don't have to have it all together — you just have to be honest about where you are.

📚 D · Merriam-Webster Word of the Day
palatable · adjective · PAL-uh-tuh-bul

To call something palatable is to say it has a pleasant or agreeable taste — or more broadly, that it is acceptable or agreeable to the mind or feelings.

"Given the traffic downtown, traveling by train is a palatable alternative to driving."
Etymology: From palate — the roof of the mouth, which the ancients considered the seat of taste and discernment. The adjective palatable emerged in English in the 17th century from a now-rare verb to palate (to taste or relish). Fun detail: palatable and plate contain the exact same letters — pure coincidence; the two words are etymologically unrelated. As M-W notes: a spoonful of sugar helps the medicine go down.

🧠 F · Britannica Encyclopaedia Fact
The Coronation That Made Britain a Television Nation 📺

When the BBC asked to televise Elizabeth II's coronation in 1953, Churchill and the Palace initially refused — fearing cameras might catch the Queen looking tired or making a mistake. Elizabeth herself overruled them.

TV set sales in Britain tripled in the months beforehand. Crowds of ten or more gathered around each set. What had been a novelty became, in one afternoon, a national institution. The coronation is widely credited as the moment that made Britain a television nation — because a 27-year-old queen trusted the medium more than her advisors did.
